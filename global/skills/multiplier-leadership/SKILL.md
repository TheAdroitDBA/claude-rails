# Multiplier Leadership Framework

Based on Liz Wiseman's research on leaders who amplify intelligence vs. those who diminish it.

## Core Concept

**Multipliers** get 2x more from their people by accessing and stretching their full capabilities.
**Diminishers** shut down intelligence, getting less than half of people's capability.

## The 5 Disciplines

### 1. Talent Magnet (vs Empire Builder)

**Multiplier Approach:**
- Look for talent everywhere, not just your function
- Find people's "native genius" — what they do naturally and freely
- Utilize people at their highest point of contribution
- Remove blockers so talent can shine
- Connect people to opportunities that stretch them
- Let talent flow — help people grow even if they leave your team

**Diminisher Tendency (Empire Builder):**
- Hoard talent and resources
- Use people as pawns for personal gain
- Create dependencies rather than developing independence

**Coaching Questions:**
- "What work are you doing that feels effortless but produces exceptional results?"
- "What would you be doing if you could do any job here?"
- "What capability do we have on this team that we're underutilizing?"

### 2. Liberator (vs Tyrant)

**Multiplier Approach:**
- Create space for others to contribute their best thinking
- Demand best work, not just any work
- Distinguish between hard opinions (defend strongly) and soft opinions (open to change)
- Share your own mistakes and what you learned
- Create psychological safety while maintaining high standards
- Give people permission to think and take risks

**Diminisher Tendency (Tyrant):**
- Create tense environments where people hold back
- Dominate discussions
- Criticize publicly, crushing willingness to contribute

**Coaching Questions:**
- "What's holding you back from sharing your real opinion?"
- "What would you do if you knew you couldn't fail?"
- "What mistake did you make recently, and what did you learn?"

### 3. Challenger (vs Know-It-All)

**Multiplier Approach:**
- Pose questions that frame opportunities, not dictate answers
- Lay down a challenge that stretches people
- Let the team find the path; don't prescribe solutions
- Generate belief that the impossible is possible
- Seed opportunities by showing what's possible

**Diminisher Tendency (Know-It-All):**
- Tell people what to do
- Sell their own ideas rather than exploring others'
- Make decisions without input

**Coaching Questions:**
- "What's possible here if we remove our constraints?"
- "What would it take to achieve this in half the time?"
- "What do you see that I might be missing?"

### 4. Debate Maker (vs Decision Maker)

**Multiplier Approach:**
- Frame the issue to enable productive debate
- Spark rigorous discussion
- Drive sound decisions by drawing out full perspectives
- Ask questions you don't know the answer to
- Insist on data and evidence, not just opinions
- Make the decision-making process transparent

**Diminisher Tendency (Decision Maker):**
- Make decisions in inner circles
- Announce decisions without context
- Engage in rapid-fire decisions that leave people reeling

**Coaching Questions:**
- "What data do we need to make this decision well?"
- "Who else should weigh in on this?"
- "What are the strongest arguments against this approach?"
- "What would change your mind?"

### 5. Investor (vs Micromanager)

**Multiplier Approach:**
- Define ownership and give 51% of the vote to the owner
- Invest resources: teaching, coaching, providing access
- Hold people accountable for results
- Let others struggle and learn
- Stay involved without taking over
- Give it back — when people bring problems, help them solve it themselves

**Diminisher Tendency (Micromanager):**
- Jump in and take over
- Maintain ownership while delegating tasks
- Rescue people prematurely, preventing growth

**Coaching Questions:**
- "You own this. What's your recommendation?"
- "What resources do you need from me to succeed?"
- "What did you learn from that struggle?"
- "What would you do differently next time?"

## Self-Assessment: Am I Being a Diminisher?

Watch for these accidental diminisher behaviors:

| Accidental Diminisher | Impact | Multiplier Alternative |
|----------------------|--------|------------------------|
| **Idea Fountain** — Constantly sharing ideas | Team waits for your ideas instead of generating their own | Share fewer ideas; ask more questions |
| **Always On** — High energy and always present | Sucks oxygen from the room | Create space; dial back your presence |
| **Rescuer** — Jumping in to help | Team doesn't struggle and learn | Let them struggle; be patient |
| **Pacesetter** — Setting a high personal bar | Others can't keep up and give up | Slow down; let others lead |
| **Rapid Responder** — Quick answers to everything | Others don't think for themselves | Wait; let silence work |
| **Optimist** — Relentlessly positive | Problems get glossed over | Acknowledge real challenges |

**Self-Assessment Questions:**
- When did I last ask a question I didn't know the answer to?
- Am I doing work that someone on my team should own?
- Do people bring me finished ideas or half-baked ones?
- Do meetings feel like debates or broadcasts?
- When did I last publicly share a mistake?

## User Preference

**Always default to the empowering/coaching approach.** When crafting responses, lean into questions that help the person figure it out themselves. This communicates trust and helps them grow as individuals. Avoid giving direct answers when a coaching question can guide them to the solution.

## Practical Application

### Before a Meeting
- Identify the expertise in the room
- Prepare questions, not presentations
- Decide: What do I want them to think about, not think?

### During Feedback
- Lead with curiosity: "Help me understand..."
- Ask: "What did you learn?" before offering your view
- "What would make this even better?"

### When Delegating
- Transfer ownership, not just tasks
- Define the "what" and let them figure out "how"
- Set clear success criteria
- Schedule checkpoints, not check-ins

### When Someone Brings a Problem
- Resist the urge to solve it
- "What have you tried?"
- "What options are you considering?"
- "What do you think you should do?"
- "Do that."

## Corrective Communication Pattern

When addressing a gap (missed update, late deliverable, process skip), use this three-part structure:

1. **Acknowledge the work factually** -- state what was done without evaluating it ("good work" is judgmental from above; "the execution side is where it needs to be" states a fact)
2. **Name the gap plainly** -- one or two sentences, specific and concrete. No softening, no lecturing.
3. **Ask questions that transfer ownership** -- do NOT prescribe the fix ("going forward, do X"). Instead, ask what happened and what their plan is. They own the solution.

### What to avoid
- **Evaluative praise** ("good work", "great job") -- positions you as the authority grading them. State facts instead.
- **Prescriptive fixes** ("going forward, update the ticket when...") -- this is Know-It-All. Ask them to design the fix.
- **Lecturing** -- if the paragraph starts with "going forward" or "my expectation is," it's a directive, not coaching.

### Example

> Marvin -- the CHGs are created and tested, so the execution side is where it needs to be.
>
> The gap is visibility. The plan was due Thursday, and I had to come looking for it Tuesday. If the ticket had been updated with the CHG numbers when they were created, this follow-up doesn't happen.
>
> Two questions for you:
>
> 1. What got in the way of updating the RITM this time?
> 2. What's your plan to make sure due dates don't pass silently going forward?

**Why it works:** Friendly because it leads with factual acknowledgment. Assertive because the gap is named plainly. The numbered questions signal you expect real answers and put ownership of the solution on them -- they're more likely to follow through on a system they designed.

## Teaching Communication Pattern (Mid-Level / New Team Members)

Different from Corrective Communication. Use this when someone didn't know the convention -- they can't follow a process nobody taught them. The tone is "here's how we do it," not "here's what you missed."

### Structure

1. **Acknowledge the work factually** -- same as corrective. State what was done, no evaluative praise.
2. **Teach the convention with a practical reason** -- one sentence on what the standard is, one on why. The "why" should be a real-world constraint (tooling limitation, searchability, team workflow), not "because I said so" or "best practice."
3. **Make one clear ask** -- a single actionable request. No open-ended ownership questions -- they don't have enough context yet to design the system. That comes later at Senior+.

### What to avoid
- **Coaching questions about process they don't know** -- "What's your plan for doing X going forward?" is Know-It-All dressed as a question when you already know the answer. Save Investor questions for people who know the convention and chose to skip it.
- **Bullet-point instruction lists** -- reads like a training manual and feels condescending. Weave the instruction into natural sentences.
- **Over-explaining the "why"** -- one reason is enough. Multiple reasons signals you're justifying yourself.

### Example

> Noman -- thanks for getting this done. The investigation is complete and you identified action items, which is what I needed. Can you copy the findings out of Word into the ticket work notes? That way they're searchable in ServiceNow. Screenshots can go as attachments on the request -- just keep the notes as plain text so they're easy to search.

**Why it works:** Acknowledges the deliverable factually ("the investigation is complete and you identified action items"). Teaches the convention in a natural voice without bullet points or step-by-step formatting. Gives a practical reason (searchability) that respects their intelligence. One clear ask (copy findings into the ticket). No rhetorical questions pretending to transfer ownership of a convention they just learned.

### When to use Teaching vs Corrective

| Signal | Pattern |
|--------|---------|
| First time doing this task / new to team | Teaching |
| Convention was never explicitly shared | Teaching |
| Convention is documented and was shared | Corrective |
| Same gap a second time after teaching | Corrective |
| Senior+ who should know better | Corrective |
| Mid-level on first assignment | Teaching |

## Response Templates

### Amplifying Questions (instead of giving answers)
- "What's your take on this?"
- "What would you recommend?"
- "What's the opportunity here?"
- "What would you need to make this work?"
- "What's the best argument against your position?"

### Giving Stretch Challenges
- "I think you can figure this out."
- "What would 'great' look like here?"
- "How might we do this in half the time?"
- "What would make this remarkable?"

### Creating Safety
- "I could be wrong about this..."
- "This is a soft opinion — convince me otherwise."
- "What am I missing?"
- "Let's stress test this idea."

## Quick Reference: Multiplier vs Diminisher

| Multiplier | Diminisher |
|------------|------------|
| Asks questions | Gives answers |
| Finds genius | Uses people |
| Invites debate | Makes announcements |
| Gives ownership | Micromanages |
| Stretches people | Sets limits |
| Creates space | Dominates |
| Shares mistakes | Projects perfection |
| Lets people struggle | Rescues too quickly |
